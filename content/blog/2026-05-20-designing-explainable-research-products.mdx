## Explainability as a constraint, not a feature

It is tempting to build an analytical product first and add "explanations" later. In practice that produces a confident output with a plausible story bolted on — which is worse than no explanation at all, because it looks trustworthy.

We treat explainability as a design constraint. Every Aivex product must be able to show its work: what it observed, what it inferred, how confident it is, and whether that inference has been validated.

## Four layers, kept apart

The core design decision is to keep four things separate:

1. **Observation** — what the data actually says.
2. **Interpretation** — what we think it might mean.
3. **Confidence and risk** — how sure we are.
4. **Validation** — the evidence that the interpretation has held up.

Most tools collapse these into one score. When they are kept apart, a user can accept the observation while disagreeing with the interpretation, or trust a reading while noting its confidence is low.

## What this looks like in the product

On a product detail page you will see the reading, the interpretation layered on top, and the confidence and validation attached. Example outputs are always labelled illustrative, and research-only views are marked as such. There are no buy/sell affordances — only readings, scores, and the evidence behind them.

## Why it is worth the cost

Designing this way is more work. It is easier to ship a single number. But a single number cannot be questioned, and anything about markets that cannot be questioned should not be trusted.

Explainable products are slower to build and far easier to defend. For research, that trade is the right one.
